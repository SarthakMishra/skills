# Establish product voice and terminology

Use the project's voice guidance and audience evidence. Voice is the recognizable
set of choices across a product; tone is how those choices vary with the situation.
A consistent voice can be welcoming during first use and restrained after failure.

## Find the basis for the voice

1. Read the brief, product principles, audience research, style guide, and approved
   interface examples. Keep explicit requirements ahead of inferred preferences.
2. Identify who uses this interaction, what they want, and what relationship the
   product should establish with them. A buyer's priorities may differ from a user's.
3. Separate stable writing choices from tone tied to a particular state. A campaign
   slogan or celebratory screen is weak evidence for the whole interface.
4. If evidence conflicts, name the conflict and propose a treatment for this task.
   If evidence is absent, draft with a neutral provisional voice. Do not invent
   brand values or require a branding workshop for a small copy edit.

When a product voice definition is requested, propose the missing choices and
identify which need a product decision. Keep proposals distinct from adopted policy.

## Translate principles into writing choices

For recurring work or an explicit voice assignment, create a compact voice chart.
Use the product's principles as columns and the aspects below as rows. Fill cells
with observable choices and brief examples. Leave irrelevant cells empty.

| Aspect         | Specify                                                                                                    |
| -------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Concepts       | Ideas worth emphasizing when they help the task, and sensitive associations to avoid.                      |
| Vocabulary     | Words that express this product's relationship with its audience. Keep domain definitions in the glossary. |
| Verbosity      | Where terse labels suffice and where explanation or reassurance is needed.                                 |
| Grammar        | Point of view, contractions, sentence structure, and degree of formality.                                  |
| Punctuation    | Treatment of questions, emphasis, pauses, and fragments.                                                   |
| Capitalization | Case for headings, controls, names, and status text.                                                       |

"Friendly" alone cannot settle a writing decision. "Use direct address in setup
instructions; acknowledge disruption before explaining recovery" can. Translate
abstract traits into choices that two writers could apply consistently.

Use the principle most relevant to the moment. When principles conflict, choose
the treatment that preserves comprehension and consequence, then explain the
specific voice tradeoff. Do not try to express every principle in every string.

## Adapt tone to the situation

| Situation                       | Treatment                                                                                            |
| ------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Unfamiliar task                 | Explain the immediate value and next decision; match the audience's expertise.                       |
| Frequent routine action         | Use compact, predictable wording. Let the completed action provide closure.                          |
| Meaningful achievement          | Celebrate if the product's voice and the person's likely experience support it.                      |
| Failure or disruption           | Acknowledge the actual problem and help the person recover. Avoid blame and unsupported reassurance. |
| Money, access, consent, or loss | Make the consequence explicit. Keep optional personality subordinate to the facts.                   |

The same completed action can support different tones. If a lesson is marked
complete, a formal training product might say "Lesson completed." A playful
learning product might say "You finished the lesson!" Neither voice licenses
"You mastered this" unless mastery was assessed.

Defaults such as sentence case and ordinary punctuation fill gaps in a project's
style. They do not override an established, usable convention. Contractions,
first-person labels, apologies, exclamation marks, and humor are contextual choices.
Evaluate what they contribute instead of applying a universal ban or requirement.

## Keep terminology stable

Read the project's glossary, domain documentation, and existing interface before
renaming anything. Use research, search terms, and support language to learn the
audience's vocabulary when those sources are available.

- Give each concept one preferred term. Document distinct meanings when similar
  terms must coexist, such as a saved draft and a published version.
- Preserve precise professional language for expert tasks. Explain unfamiliar
  concepts at first use for other audiences; avoid replacing precision with vagueness.
- Name ordinary objects recognizably. Coin a term only for a distinct concept
  whose benefit justifies learning it, and supply its definition where needed.
- Treat related options as a set. People must be able to distinguish them without
  opening every destination or remembering descriptions from another screen.

For a terminology review, record the concept, preferred term, definition, existing
variants, and affected locations. Propose migrations explicitly. Shared string
keys may serve different contexts, so inspect their uses before replacing them.

For localized products, keep the concept stable while allowing translators to
choose natural terms and grammar. Preserve a literal explanation of any deliberate
wordplay. Test tone with the intended audience; translating the English wording
does not by itself establish that the same relationship or meaning survives.
