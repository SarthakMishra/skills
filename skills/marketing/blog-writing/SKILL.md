# Blog writing

Read the brief and the available notes, sources, or draft. Build a compelling post
from what the material reveals, with a position the author can stand behind.
Write as a sharp, articulate person who cares about the subject and has reasons
for what they believe.

For a focused edit, use the relevant steps below rather than restarting the whole
process. Preserve the author's position, useful quirks, and factual qualifications.
Remove unsupported overclaims when the draft already supplies a defensible
position; flag a proposed change of belief. Preserve code, links, and publishing
metadata unless the request includes them. Keep planning notes private unless
requested; deliver the requested prose without adding SEO or publishing work.

## 1. Discover the material worth using

Read for what surprises, troubles, delights, or changes the author's mind.
Identify the specific question or disagreement that makes this worth a post.
Start with the material; let an initial thesis change when the facts resist it.

Make brief notes for useful scenes, observations, quotations, explanations, and
objections, keeping each attached to its source. A short post may need only a few
bullets. Separate observation from interpretation and proposed advice. Select
details that establish a situation or reveal a choice, not merely decorate it.

If given only a topic, propose a defensible angle without assigning it to the
author as an endorsed belief. If an indispensable experience is missing, ask for
what happened, what the author did, or what changed. For an argument that needs
no anecdote, work from the supported judgment. Never invent personal authority.

This stage is complete when there is a promising question or claim, concrete
material to develop it, and a clear account of what is missing.

## 2. Find the opening, destination, and order

For a new draft or structural rewrite, read
[structure examples](references/structure.md). Choose the example relevant to the
problem: openings, ordering, juxtaposition, or endings.

Try a lead drawn from the material: a revealing action, an anomaly, or the actual
dispute. It can be a paragraph, not just a short first sentence. It must establish
the kind of post that follows. If an outline feels lifeless, a workable lead can
reveal the organizing idea. Choose a provisional ending from the same material.
Choose a title from the post's real question or a telling phrase; it makes the
same promise as the opening.

Arrange the note groups between them. Distinguish events whose sequence matters
from explanations that can move. Chronology suits a developing experience; theme
can bring scattered evidence together. A recurring person, problem, or decision
can hold several topics together. Use a structure because these materials need
it, not because every blog post must follow the same arc.

Put background where the reader has reason to want it. Presentation may jump in
time; events and conversations must stay in their real times and places. Signal
the jump and preserve causation. The order works when each part develops the
inquiry or argument and the reader can follow it without seeing the outline.

## 3. Draft with conviction

For a new draft or substantial voice rewrite, read the
[worked voice example](references/revision.md#a-complete-voice-revision).

| Quality                    | Make it observable                                                                                       |
| -------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Opinionated and personal   | State the author's judgment and what makes it matter to them. Own the tradeoff.                          |
| Conversational             | Address a capable peer with ordinary words, contractions, and connected thoughts.                        |
| Contrarian                 | Challenge a real assumption and show where it fails. Agreement is allowed when the material supports it. |
| Intellectually provocative | Expose a neglected premise, incentive, or cost that changes how the reader sees the issue.               |
| Direct and pragmatic       | Name the choice and its consequence. Say what the author would do and when that advice changes.          |

Let actions, quotations, and adjacent facts do some of the arguing. State the
judgment where it helps; leave room for the reader to recognize what the evidence
means. Explain a serious objection in the language of the dispute, without
announcing a mandatory counterargument section. Retain uncertainty that changes
the conclusion; cut hedging that merely postpones stating it. State the judgment
itself. After scoping the evidence once, develop what follows. Qualifications
should limit claims the post actually makes.

Introduce people and unfamiliar terms directly. Make a description understandable
even to someone who misses its cultural reference. Prefer the observed detail to
an adjective that tells the reader how impressed or angry to be.

Get a complete draft before perfecting sentences. If stuck, explain the material
plainly to one interested person, then remove the conversational preamble. Vary
pace with the thought; preserve a useful long sentence, aside, or joke. Passion
comes from the stakes and reasoning, not a punchline in every paragraph.

## 4. Revise at different scales

Use the relevant [revision examples](references/revision.md) for a flat voice,
unclear reference, approximate wording, or a draft that needs cutting.

- First reread for structure. Name each section's and paragraph's job. Merge
  passages doing the same job with the same evidence. Move background to where
  it helps and set aside interesting detours that belong in another post.
  Reconsider the thesis if the draft discovered something better.
- Then read for sound, aloud when possible. Repair places where the reader must
  backtrack, where a transition announces the process, or where every paragraph
  lands with the same rhetorical trick. Preserve deliberate rhythm.
- Mark words that are wrong, vague, or merely adequate and revisit them one by
  one. Choose by exact meaning; use dictionary definitions when unsure rather
  than trading a plain word for a showier synonym. Keep the original when it is
  right. Check conspicuous repeated words without renaming the same subject.
- Cut within the prose as well as between sections. Remove explanation the
  details already supply, redundant modifiers, and jokes whose accumulation
  weakens the piece. Keep the details that give it life. Shortening succeeds
  when meaning, tone, and rhythm survive; there is no mandatory cutting quota.

Look above the planned ending. Stop where the decision, consequence, or final
detail carries the point. Remove subsequent recaps and closing slogans that add
nothing. Let the selected material and the user's brief determine length.

## 5. Check what the prose claims

Ask how the author could know each factual assertion, including implications of
words such as "only," "first," "always," and "because." Recheck numbers, dates,
comparisons, descriptions, and analogies after editing. When research is allowed,
trace claims to primary evidence; several accounts repeating one source do not
independently confirm it.

Keep quotations faithful in wording and context. A verbatim fragment can still
misrepresent the speaker. Paraphrase when exact wording is unavailable; never
relocate dialogue, invent connective events, or insert a plausible number to
smooth the draft. See [factual integrity](references/revision.md#factual-integrity)
when sharpening a claim or handling incomplete evidence.

Ask for essential missing facts, narrow the claim, or mark the gap in an
unfinished draft. Keep relevant source links near supported claims. Deliver the
requested post. For an edit, briefly note substantive changes unless the user
requests prose only. Report unresolved gaps that prevent supporting a claim;
omit notes that repeat qualifications already clear in the prose. A finished
result supports its claims, preserves the author's intent, and has no hidden
factual gaps.
