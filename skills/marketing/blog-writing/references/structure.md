# Structure examples

Use these examples when choosing an opening, arranging notes, connecting sections,
or finding an ending. All situations and quotations here are invented teaching
material. Treat each example's supplied facts as its source of truth. The examples
show decisions, not structures every post must imitate.

## Select material before settling the message

Material: an author planned a side project for six months, then changed the plan
after a new client deadline arrived three weeks later. Making the plan had already
revealed that the author had promised more work than they could finish.

Bad, forcing the material into a predetermined position:

> Planning is a waste of time. My six-month plan lasted three weeks, which proves
> that trying to predict the future is pointless.

Good, finding the distinction the material supports:

> Three weeks in, I had to change my six-month plan. It had already done something useful:
> shown me I'd promised more work than I could finish. I still want a plan. I want
> to be able to change it when the work changes.

The good version retains the inconvenient benefit instead of discarding it to
protect a hot take. The developing question is what a plan should be used for.
An opinion can become sharper by becoming more exact.

## Write an opening that promises the actual post

Material: a documentation typo spent forty-eight hours in a review queue. The
correction took four minutes. Three approvals were required. The post questions
whether small corrections need that policy; the notes do not establish what
caused the delay.

Bad, announcing a topic:

> Approval processes play an important role in modern organizations. This post
> explores their advantages and disadvantages.

Bad, promising drama the material cannot deliver:

> One tiny mistake exposed the fatal flaw destroying our entire industry.

Good, opening with the scale of the actual problem:

> A typo waited forty-eight hours for approval. Fixing it took four minutes.
> The policy required three approvals. What judgment did each one need to add?

This beginning supplies a concrete situation and an honest question. The body
now owes the reader an examination of that policy. It does not owe an industry
exposé or proof that the reviewers caused the delay. The opening can occupy
several sentences; its job is to establish interest and direction.
A matching title would be "Why does a typo need three approvals?"

## Arrange the same notes in different ways

Material for a post about a troublesome data export:

| Note | Established material                                                              | Source                     |
| ---- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------- |
| A    | On Monday the author selected a service for its custom fields.                    | Author's notes             |
| B    | The product page said, "Export your data whenever you want."                      | Saved page                 |
| C    | On Friday the author tested export. It downloaded rows but omitted custom fields. | Test file                  |
| D    | Support confirmed those fields needed a separate API request.                     | Support reply              |
| E    | The author had not tested export before committing to the service.                | Author's notes             |
| F    | The author now wants to test required fields before committing to a service.      | Author's endorsed judgment |

Bad, using the order in which research was collected as the article's order:

1. A history of export formats.
2. The service's complete feature list.
3. Notes A through F, in that order.
4. A general conclusion about data ownership.

Good, choosing order around the discovery:

1. C: Friday's export is missing the feature Monday's decision depended on.
2. A and E: "Four days earlier..." establishes the original decision and the
   author's untested assumption.
3. B beside C: compare the promise with the actual file.
4. D: explain the separate API route and what that changes about the assessment.
5. F: finish with the author's decision to test required fields before committing.

The event sequence remains Monday then Friday. Presentation starts on Friday and
marks the return to Monday. B and the API explanation can move; the actual events
cannot. Keeping D prevents an easier but false story that the fields are trapped.

A thematic version could instead examine what "export" includes, how much work
recovery takes, and what to test before committing, using these same notes. Choose
it when those questions matter more than the author's discovery over time. A
short post can simply move from the failed test to its consequence. Outline only
as much as the material needs.

For a larger draft, give each note a short label and keep its source. Group by
the jobs above, then write from the current group's notes. That makes it harder
to bury an observation under everything else the research happened to uncover.

## Let juxtaposition carry meaning

Material: a team says its highest priority is quick correction of documentation
errors. Its policy requires three approvals even for a typo. Both facts are
established; no one has explained why the policy exists.

Bad, explaining the desired reaction and inventing a motive:

> This is a deeply hypocritical organization. Management obviously values control
> over quality and has no interest in letting people get things done.

Good, placing the relevant facts together:

> The team calls quick correction its highest priority. A typo still needs three
> approvals.

The reader can see the tension without the author announcing hypocrisy. The
next paragraph can investigate what the approvals contribute. A firm judgment
about the policy can follow; a claim about management's private motives needs
evidence of its own. Space between telling details can do more than another
sentence explaining their moral.

## Find the ending inside the material

Material: the author still uses a planning document but no longer treats its
task order as a promise. When a new constraint arrives, they change the task
order and check whether the client delivery date still holds.

Bad, appending a summary after reaching the decision:

> I still make the plan. When a new constraint arrives, I change the task order
> and check whether the delivery date still holds.
>
> Ultimately, planning is a complex topic with many perspectives. We have seen
> the importance of adaptability, communication, and balance. What are your
> thoughts on planning?

Good, ending where the decision becomes clear:

> I still make the plan. When a new constraint arrives, I change the task order
> and check whether the delivery date still holds.

The better ending already existed above the planned wrap-up. A final scene,
quotation, or changed understanding can also close a post when it carries the
meaning. Choose what this material has earned; do not add an ornamental last
line merely to make it quotable.
