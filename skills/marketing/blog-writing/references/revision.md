# Voice and revision examples

Use the complete revision to calibrate a new draft or substantial voice rewrite.
Use the later examples for description, word choice, cutting, and factual checks.
These are invented teaching situations, not facts or personal history to reuse.

## A complete voice revision

Material supplied by the fictional author:

- They read for pleasure and set a target of twenty-four books in January.
- In March they put down a six-hundred-page novel ninety pages in, while enjoying
  it, because its length would make the annual target harder to reach.
- They later returned to the novel, finished it, and enjoyed it.
- They still keep a list of books they want to read, but have dropped the target.
- They accept that a quota can help someone establish a reading habit.
- They have not measured whether they read more or less since dropping it.

Bad, vague reflection followed by an unsupported universal:

> Reading goals can be a powerful tool for personal growth. I set myself a goal
> of twenty-four books, and this journey taught me valuable lessons. In March,
> I put down a long novel after ninety pages. Later, I went back and finished it.
> This highlights the importance of balance. Everyone should stop counting books
> and focus on meaningful reading. Ultimately, it is important to find what works
> for you.

Good, a complete short post built from the same material:

> I put down a novel I was enjoying because it was too long.
>
> I was ninety pages into a six-hundred-page book. Back in January, I'd set myself
> a target of twenty-four books for the year. By March, the length of a book I
> liked had become a reason to stop reading it. A shorter book would have helped
> the count.
>
> When I went back and finished the novel later, I still enjoyed it.
>
> If a quota gets you to open a book, keep it. That is a useful thing for a target
> to do. Mine had started interfering with the pleasure I was reading for. I don't
> want to hurry through a long novel just to count it as one.
>
> I still keep a list of books I want to read. The annual target is gone. I can't
> tell you whether dropping it has made me read more. I can tell you that I want
> to finish a book I'm enjoying, however long it takes.

The good version has a position, a cost, and a decision. Its disagreement concerns
what the number was encouraging the author to do. It makes no claim that quotas
fail everyone. The concession belongs inside the reasoning, without a label
announcing a counterargument. The limit on measured results appears once where
it matters. Short sentences sit among developed thoughts rather than standing
in for them.

The first-person detail earns the conviction. Without that supplied experience,
the post would need a different basis. The lesson is the relation between
material and judgment, not the exact opener, paragraph count, or closing rhythm.

Bad, announcing the argument at arm's length:

> My argument is about whether reading goals may have drawbacks.

Good, stating the judgment the material supports:

> I don't want a target that gives me a reason to put down a book I'm enjoying.

Once the scope is clear, develop the consequence. Repeating the same caveat
or describing the argument again makes the post longer without making it fairer.

## Make a reference earn its place

Material: the fictional author cannot log into their account as a customer. The
cancellation form is available only inside it. The intended reader does not need
a literary reference to understand the problem.

Bad, making the reader supply the description:

> The cancellation process was Kafkaesque.

Good, supplying what actually happened:

> To cancel, I had to fill out a form inside the account I couldn't log into.

This makes the obstruction visible even to someone who misses the allusion.
If a reference adds wit or meaning, it can remain beside a description that
already works. Apply the same test to an unexplained acronym or a comparison
whose familiar name is doing all the descriptive work.

## Choose the word by what happened

Material: an export the fictional author uses finishes every time, but takes
twelve minutes. They have to click a button to start it each time. There is no
evidence of failures.

Bad, choosing forceful words without checking their meaning:

> The unreliable export was finally automated.

Good, choosing words the evidence supports:

> The export works, but I still have to start it myself and wait twelve minutes.

"Unreliable" invents a failure pattern; "automated" invents a change. Even
"slow" is less informative here than the observed wait. In a late pass, mark
these approximate words, consider what each needs to convey, and check a
definition when meanings are close. Replacing them with grander synonyms would
leave the factual problem intact.

The same pass can improve a word that is already accurate. With notes saying
that two unfinished tasks moved from Monday to Friday, "I revised the plan" is
correct but broad. "I moved the unfinished tasks to Friday" names the actual
revision. The better wording answers a useful question rather than displaying
a larger vocabulary.

Keep a distinctive word when it is exact. If the draft calls everything
"absurd," decide which observation deserves it and replace other uses with what
happened. Keep the same name for the export throughout; variety does not require
calling it an engine, a system, and a solution in successive sentences.

## Cut without removing the writer

Material: the fictional author spent four minutes fixing a typo, then waited
forty-eight hours for three approvals. Their irritation concerns the difference
between the edit and the wait, not a measured organization-wide pattern.

Before, with repetition and filler:

> I spent four minutes fixing the typo. Then I spent forty-eight hours waiting
> for three approvals. I kept thinking about how strange that was. The correction
> took less time than making a cup of tea, but the waiting took two whole days.
> There were three different people who had to approve it. I don't think a typo
> should need a committee.

Bad cut, retaining data but flattening the thought:

> Typo: 4m. Approval: 48h. Reviewers: 3. Process inefficient.

Good cut, preserving the complaint and its rhythm:

> I spent four minutes fixing the typo, then forty-eight hours waiting for three
> approvals. I don't think a typo should need a committee.

The good cut removes repeated information across the paragraph. It keeps the
specific contrast, first-person irritation, and a sentence that owns the
judgment. Cutting the entire final sentence would make it shorter while losing
part of the author's point. On a longer post, protect the small collection of
details that establishes its setting and people as well as its argument.

## Factual integrity

### A sharper opinion can keep a narrower fact

Material: one typo correction waited forty-eight hours under a three-approval
policy. There is no study of the team's other work or a record of what caused the
wait. The author's judgment is that each required approval should have a reason.

Bad, turning an observation into a causal finding:

> Three reviewers make every correction take two days.

Good, separating the observation from the recommendation:

> One typo waited two days under a three-approval policy. I want a reason for
> each required approval.

The limited observation is explicit without a separate defensive paragraph. It
still permits a firm policy question. Check small words too: "every" broadens
the sample and "make" claims causation. The same
scrutiny applies to "only," "first," dates, quantities, and implications tucked
into descriptions. Two articles repeating this one incident remain one incident.

### Exact fragments can misrepresent a source

Recorded statement:

> For urgent incidents, call me. For routine updates, leave a note.

Bad, quoting exact words with the condition removed:

> She said, "call me," confirming that she wants updates by phone.

Good, preserving what the speaker distinguished:

> She wants calls for urgent incidents and notes for routine updates.

The good version uses paraphrase and preserves the meaning. If the original
wording matters, quote the relevant complete statement instead. Keep separate
conversations in their original settings; a smoother invented conversation is
still invented.

### Ask for the missing experience

Request: "Write about why I quit social media," with no reasons or events supplied.

Bad, supplying a plausible personal scene:

> One night, I looked up from my phone and realized I'd missed dinner with my
> family again.

Good, eliciting the material the story needs:

> What made you quit? Tell me about the change you noticed or an event that
> captures it. Rough notes are enough.

If the answer is a gradual change, develop that rather than demanding a dramatic
incident. When exact words, dates, or measurements are missing, obtain them,
paraphrase within what is known, or leave a visible gap in an unfinished draft.
Never let a plausible placeholder become an unnoticed fact.
